# Visuals and rich moments

Decorative filler is slop. A visual that belongs to the product is craft.

## Decide whether a visual is needed
- Most app screens, settings, and dense tools do not need a decorative hero.
- Landing heroes and meaningful empty states usually do.
- When the brief calls for high-budget presence, one strong moment (3D object, shader field, interactive scene, or signature type treatment) is usually enough. Do not decorate everything.

## What to show
1. Prefer a scene or object that comes from the product’s world.
2. Or show the product itself: a carefully framed preview of the actual interface or data that proves the headline.
3. Write the scene as one plain sentence before generating or building anything. If you cannot say it in one sentence, it is not clear yet.

Examples of good sentence shape (do not reuse — derive from the current product):
- A tool that protects backups → a small lighthouse on a dark rocky coast with its beam reaching into fog
- A logistics platform → one container ship seen from above on a calm grey sea
- A meditation product → a single cup of tea steaming on a windowsill at first light

Avoid AI defaults unless the product is literally about them: glossy blobs, orbs, nebulas, chrome liquid, floating cubes, neon circuits, holograms, glowing brains, robots, rockets, crystal shapes.

## High-craft techniques (use when the domain and budget support them)
These are tools for a rich moment, not defaults:
- **Purposeful 3D / interactive object** — a single model or scene that the user can orbit, scrub, or that reacts to scroll/cursor. Prefer one hero object over a room full of floating things.
- **Shader / generative fields** — noise, fluid, displacement, or logo treatments that create depth and motion without looking like stock “AI background.”
- **Signature type moment** — large, carefully set display type that carries hierarchy and personality (maximalist or restrained depending on the direction).
- **Tactile / material language** — paper grain, print texture, soft gradients, or physical product photography that makes the interface feel made rather than generated.

Any of the above must still pass the domain-native and anti-slop tests. A 3D car on a logistics dashboard is usually wrong; a precise route diagram or fleet status object may be right.

## Reference mixing (direction technique)
A powerful way to raise craft is to take the real product and deliberately mix it with a clear cultural or graphic reference, then resolve the collision into one coherent direction. Examples of the *method* (not recipes to copy):
- Flat / corporate site + strong material or glass language → depth without clutter
- Unmemorable layout + Swiss poster discipline → hierarchy and restraint
- Cluttered site + bold typographic maximalism → one dominant type system
- Empty site + tactile / texture language → presence without noise
- Predictable SaaS + a specific era or cultural graphic system → memorable character

The reference is a catalyst. The final result must still belong to the product’s world and survive the industry recognition test.

## Styles that work
Pick one that fits the character and keep it consistent:
- Painted / illustrated background with calm open space for type
- Cinematic photo of one real object
- Precise product UI preview with plausible data
- Technical drawing / diagram language when the domain is industrial or scientific
- Controlled 3D / shader moment when the product warrants presence

## The rich moment
Spend the visual budget on one (or a few tightly related) moments only this product could own. Keep the chrome quiet. Let the signature element be memorable; everything around it stays disciplined.
